# What to include on a trades business website — the essentials

If you run a trades business in Ireland — plumber, electrician, builder, roofer, landscaper, groundworker — your website has one job: turn visitors into phone calls or quote requests. That is the whole test. Nothing about awards, aesthetics, or brand personality matters if the phone is not ringing.

This post is a practical list of what an Irish trades business website actually needs to achieve that, ordered roughly by importance. No theory, no SEO jargon. Just the pages, content, and features that move the needle.

## The shape of the visitor

Before any checklist, picture the visitor. Someone in your catchment, on a phone, usually with a problem that needs solving soon. They found you via Google, maybe a neighbour's recommendation, maybe your van. They have about 30 seconds of attention. If the site answers their three questions — can you do my job, are you trustworthy, how do I contact you — they will contact you. If it does not, they will click back and try the next result.

Everything below serves those 30 seconds.

## 1. The phone number, prominently

On every page, at the top, tappable on mobile. No clever hiding. No "get in touch" button that hides the number behind a form. The phone number is the single highest-value piece of the site for a trades business.

If you take calls through a specific mobile, put that. If you have a landline, put both. Add a "tap to call" mobile link so pressing the number dials it. This is 90% of the UX of a trades website in one design decision.

## 2. Clear service list

What you do, in the visitor's language. Not "integrated mechanical solutions" — "bathroom plumbing, central heating, leak repair, boiler service." Short. Bulleted. Visible on the homepage without scrolling.

If you cover multiple areas, list them. If you are specialised, say so clearly. Visitors should not have to guess whether you do what they need.

## 3. Coverage area

The towns, counties, or regions you cover. "Based in Portlaoise, serving Laois, Carlow, and Kilkenny" in plain text on the homepage and in a small line under the phone number. This does two things:

- Tells visitors whether you are even relevant to them.
- Helps Google rank you for local searches in those towns.

If you cover all of Ireland, say so. If you only cover a specific radius, say so. The visitor from outside your area can then move on; the visitor inside it knows they have the right business.

## 4. Testimonials — real ones, with names

Short quotes from recent clients with their first name and town. Two or three on the homepage, more on a reviews page. Ideally with star ratings pulled from Google reviews so visitors can click through and verify.

What not to do: fake testimonials with stock photos, testimonials with no name, testimonials from 2017. Visitors have a reliable filter for these, and they hurt rather than help.

## 5. Photos of your work

Not stock photos of generic construction sites. Your actual work. Before and after photos if the nature of the work lends itself. Photos of your team on the job. Photos of your van. These do two things — they prove you are a real business with real results, and they give Google images to associate with your services.

Photos do not need to be professional. A good phone photo in good light beats a stock image every time. Watermark them with your business name if you want; do not obsess about it.

## 6. About page with a face

A page with the owner's name, a photo, a short biography, and what the business stands for. Two or three paragraphs. This matters especially for trades where the relationship is personal — the visitor wants to know who is going to turn up at their house.

If you have a small team, include them. If you are a sole trader, own it. Either is fine. What is not fine is a generic "Our Story" page full of "we are passionate about customer service."

## 7. Quote request form that works

A short form — name, phone, email, a short note on what they need. Three to five fields maximum. Long forms with twenty fields convert at a fraction of short ones.

The form should email or SMS you the moment it is submitted. A form that sits in a database nobody checks is worse than no form. Respond within business hours, same day where possible.

## 8. Credentials and insurances, stated plainly

RECI membership for electricians. RGII for gas installers. Safe Pass for construction work. Public liability and employer's liability insurance, with cover amounts. Any specific industry memberships.

A simple line on the homepage — "Fully insured. RECI registered. Safe Pass." — answers a question many visitors ask and some do not bother to ask, assuming the worst.

## 9. Pricing or pricing signals

A full price list is often not practical for trades — each job is different. But some pricing signal helps: a starting call-out fee, an indicative hourly rate, typical project ranges for common work. "Bathroom renovations typically €7,000 - €15,000 depending on spec." Something.

This filters visitors in both directions. Those who cannot afford you move on and do not waste your time. Those who can afford you feel confident you are not going to spring a surprise.

## 10. Local SEO basics

Not a rabbit hole; a checklist.

- Google Business Profile claimed, photos up, opening hours correct, reviews gathered over time.
- NAP (name, address, phone) consistent across the site, Google, and any directory listings.
- Town/county names used naturally in page copy.
- A service area page or two for major towns if you cover a wide area.
- Photos uploaded to Google Business Profile as well as the site.

Our [page speed for local business websites](/blog/page-speed-local-business-websites) post covers the technical side.

## 11. Fast, mobile-first pages

Over 70% of trades-website traffic is on mobile. Pages that take five seconds to load lose half their visitors before the phone number is even visible. Targets:

- Under 3 seconds to first meaningful content.
- Images optimised and appropriately sized.
- No autoplay video on the homepage.
- Tap targets big enough for fingers.

Most modern templates get this right. The common failure is installing 12 plugins and uploading un-sized images. Keep it simple.

## 12. A privacy notice and basic GDPR

If the site has a contact form, it collects personal data. You need a short privacy notice explaining what happens to that data, a cookie banner if you use tracking tools, and a process to delete personal data on request. These are legal requirements under GDPR in Ireland and are enforced.

Not a big job — a one-page privacy notice and a compliant cookie banner. Any decent web provider will set this up as standard.

## What to skip

Features that get sold to trades businesses and usually do not help:

- **Elaborate homepage animations.** Slow the page down, distract from the phone number.
- **Chatbots.** A chatbot replying at 10pm is not useful. A call-back request is.
- **Hero videos.** Pretty, heavy, usually ignored.
- **Blog posts written by an AI once a week with no human in the loop.** Embarrassing and noticed. If you do blog, blog rarely and well.
- **Fancy parallax scrolling.** Looks dated, hurts mobile performance.

None of these move the needle on phone calls. All of them cost money and time.

## A realistic scope for a trades website

For most Irish trades businesses, a sensible website is five to eight pages:

1. Home
2. About
3. Services (or one page per major service)
4. Service areas (towns/counties)
5. Gallery or Case studies
6. Reviews
7. Contact
8. Privacy / terms

That is enough to rank well, present the business clearly, and convert visitors into enquiries. More pages do not make the business look bigger; they often make it harder to find what matters.

## Care plan afterwards

A site that works on launch day will not work a year later without some maintenance. Plugins and themes need updating, SSL certificates renewing, backups running, broken links catching.
