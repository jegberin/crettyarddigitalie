# Why cyber awareness training matters for SMEs — and how to do it well

For a small business in Ireland, the cheapest and most effective cybersecurity control is a staff team that knows what phishing looks like, understands why MFA (multi-factor authentication — that second code on your phone when you log in) matters, and knows who to tell when something looks wrong. No paid product beats that for return on money spent.

Unfortunately, most "cyber awareness training" does not achieve this. It is either a once-a-year compliance video nobody remembers, or an expensive platform that sends endless simulated phishing emails and turns the whole topic into background noise. This post is about why the training matters and how to run it so it actually changes behaviour.

## What awareness training is really for

Two things. One, to reduce the chance a staff member clicks on something harmful. Two, to increase the chance they speak up quickly when something looks suspicious.

Both of those are behaviour changes, not knowledge transfers. A staff member who can recite the definition of phishing but still clicks on a convincing email has not benefited from training. A staff member who cannot define phishing but checks with the manager before clicking anything unusual has.

Everything about how the training is designed should flow from that framing.

## Why it punches above its weight

Almost every major incident at an SME (small or medium-sized enterprise) I see in Ireland is, at some step in its chain, a human one. The phishing email that got clicked. The password that was reused. The staff member who authorised a supplier bank change over email without phoning. The junior who gave their password to "IT support" over the phone because they were panicking about a deadline.

Good technical controls reduce the frequency of these moments. Good training reduces the chance they become incidents when they happen. The two work together and neither replaces the other.

The specific scenarios training helps with:

- **Phishing emails** — noticing the signs, not clicking, reporting.
- **Business email compromise** — checking bank details by phone, not email.
- **Password behaviour** — using the password manager, not reusing.
- **MFA fatigue attacks** — knowing never to approve a prompt you did not initiate.
- **Tailgating and pretexting** — physical and phone-based social engineering.
- **AI misuse** — not pasting sensitive data into public chatbots.

## What good training looks like

Four characteristics tend to separate training that works from training that does not.

### 1. Short and frequent

The ideal is 20 minutes every six months, not a two-hour marathon once a year. The reason is simple — memory decays. A short, high-engagement session twice a year, with live examples from the last quarter, leaves a much stronger residue than a comprehensive annual module.

### 2. Specific to your business

Generic training uses generic examples. "A fake Microsoft email might say there's a problem with your account." Specific training uses your examples. "Here's the phishing email the finance team received last month. Here's what made it convincing. Here's what the tell was."

The latter works because it is concrete and because it connects to the actual stakes of your business. Phishing emails pretending to be from *your* bank, *your* accountant, *your* clients.

### 3. Live, not pre-recorded, where possible

A live session — even on Teams — with a real person walking through real examples, taking questions, and responding to the room, beats any recorded video. The engagement is different. People remember what they laughed at, what surprised them, what they argued about. That is what sticks.

For remote teams, 30 minutes on Teams with the camera on beats a training platform any day.

### 4. Paired with an easy way to report

Training people to recognise phishing is useless if reporting it is hard. Every staff member should know, in one click, how to flag a suspicious email. The Microsoft 365 Report Message button, or an internal mailbox like `phishing@yourfirm.ie`, both work. What matters is that the path is obvious and frictionless.

When staff report a suspicious email, reply with thanks. Every time. If they were right, praise. If they were wrong, explain why it was in fact legitimate. This creates a culture where reporting is rewarded — even when the report turned out to be a false alarm.

## Topics worth covering

A half-day of content for a typical SME, split across two or three sessions per year, should cover:

1. **How phishing actually works**, with live examples. What attackers want and how they try to get it.
2. **MFA** — why it matters, how to use Authenticator with number matching, never approve unexpected prompts.
3. **Passwords and the password manager** — how to use it, what "unique per site" means, what to do if a password is breached.
4. **Business email compromise** — bank detail changes, invoice redirection, how to verify by phone.
5. **What to do if you think you clicked on something** — who to tell, no blame, speed matters.
6. **AI use** — what to never paste into public AI tools, approved tools and settings. More in our [AI policy basics for SMEs](/blog/ai-policy-basics-smes) post.
7. **Physical security and visitors** — not letting strangers into the office, not leaving laptops in cars.
8. **Data protection basics** — GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation — the EU data protection law) in plain English, what "personal data" means for your role, who the data protection lead is.

The mix depends on the business. An accountancy firm spends more on BEC (business email compromise — attackers posing as staff or suppliers to redirect payments). A retailer spends more on payment fraud. A medical clinic spends more on data protection.

## Phishing simulations — use sparingly and kindly

Simulated phishing campaigns — where you or your provider send fake phishing emails to see who clicks — can complement training if used thoughtfully.

**Useful approach:** once or twice a year, with a realistic but fair email, followed by a short constructive note to anyone who clicked ("this is what you were seeing; here is what to look for next time") and no punishment.

**Not useful approach:** monthly simulations that staff come to dread, overly convincing "gotcha" emails impersonating trusted colleagues, naming and shaming, or tying results to performance reviews. All of these generate resentment and reduce reporting, which is the opposite of what you want.

Microsoft 365 Defender's Attack Simulation Training is free on most Business Premium plans and produces decent simulations for a baseline. Third-party tools add variety if you need more.

## Measuring whether it is working

Awareness is hard to measure precisely, but there are proxies that tell you directionally:

- **Phishing reports per month** — should go up after training, especially initially.
- **Click-through on simulated phishing** — should trend down over time.
- **Incidents caused by staff error** — should drop.
- **Questions asked** — a team that is engaged asks more questions, not fewer.

If the numbers are flat after two cycles, the training is not landing. Change the format or the content.

## What to avoid

Common mistakes to skip:

- **Punitive culture around clicking.** If staff are afraid to admit they clicked, they will not tell you. That silence is what an attacker needs to keep working.
- **Fear-based messaging.** "One click could destroy the business" is true but does not change behaviour; it just makes people anxious and avoidant.
- **Outsourcing entirely to a platform.** Platforms can supplement a live session. They cannot replace one.
- **Assuming senior staff already know.** Owners and managers click on phishing at similar rates to other staff; sometimes higher, because they see more email.
