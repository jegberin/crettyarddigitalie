# Signs your business website is quietly costing you leads

Most SME websites don't fail loudly. They just quietly underperform — ten potential enquiries a month become three, and nobody joins the dots. The site technically works, the owner has seen it on their phone once, and so the problem never gets named. But the cost is real, and it compounds month after month.

This is a plain list of the signs I watch for when a business tells me their website "isn't bringing in much". None of them are cosmetic. Each one maps to an action you can take in the next fortnight.

## 1. It takes more than three seconds to load on mobile

Speed is the first filter. If a visitor lands on your homepage from a Google search on a 4G phone and nothing useful is on screen by the three-second mark, a sizeable share of them bounce before they've seen your logo. The pattern is well-documented in Google's own performance research and in the Core Web Vitals standards used for ranking.

A few common culprits on Irish SME sites:

- Hero images uploaded straight from a DSLR at 6MB each
- A heavy page builder loading ten fonts the design doesn't actually use
- Third-party chat widgets and cookie banners that block rendering
- A cheap shared hosting plan doing its honest best for €3 a month

The fix is usually not a rebuild. It's image compression, removing what you don't need, and moving to hosting that's fit for purpose. That's a conversation worth having before you spend five figures on a redesign.

## 2. The homepage doesn't say what you do, for whom, in the first screen

Open your site on a phone. Hold it at arm's length. Can a stranger tell — in three seconds, without scrolling — what service you provide and who you provide it to? If they can't, you're asking every visitor to work harder than they will.

The fix is usually one clear sentence at the top. "Plumbing and heating services for homes and businesses in Kilkenny and South Laois." "Bookkeeping and payroll for Irish SMEs under 20 staff." Specific, human, and written in the language your customers already use. Marketing copy that tries to sound clever at the top of the page almost always costs you enquiries.

## 3. There's no obvious next step

Visitors convert when the next step is easy and visible. Too many SME websites make the visitor hunt for it. The contact page is three clicks away, the phone number is in a small footer, and the form has nine fields when it only needs three.

A decent site should have:

- A phone number in the top-right of every page, clickable on mobile
- A short contact form on the homepage itself, not buried
- A single, unambiguous call-to-action per section — "Request a quote", "Book a free call", "See pricing"

If you want a concrete benchmark, look at how the [get a quote flow](/get-a-quote) or [contact form](/contact) sits on our own site. The goal is never to be clever; it's to make the next action so obvious a tired visitor on a train can take it.

## 4. Nobody can find you on Google for what you actually do

Search engines are still where most Irish SMEs get found. Two quick tests:

1. Open an incognito tab and Google your service plus your town — "accountant Carlow", "emergency plumber Portlaoise", "wedding photographer Kilkenny". Are you on page one?
2. Do the same for two surrounding towns. How far do you rank?

If you're missing from both, the problem is usually a mix of weak on-page content (the words on your pages don't match what customers type) and a missing or stale Google Business Profile. Neither is expensive to fix. Both take a few hours of focused work.

Local service pages — the kind we build for [web design in Laois](/web-design-laois), [Carlow](/web-design-carlow), and [Kilkenny](/web-design-kilkenny) — are another lever, provided they're written for humans first and crawlers second. A page that repeats the town name 40 times looks like what it is.

## 5. The site hasn't been touched in more than a year

This one is less about design and more about trust. A page with a 2021 copyright notice, a "book now for summer 2022" banner, and staff who left eighteen months ago is a signal to any careful buyer that you don't mind about the details. That signal is more damaging than an old typeface.

Regular, light upkeep — a monthly pass by somebody who knows what they're looking at — catches most of this. That's the core of a decent [website care plan](/website-care-plans): small, ongoing, boring work that stops the site from drifting into embarrassment.

## 6. The forms don't actually work

I see this every month. Somebody changes a DNS record, or the SMTP password rotates, or an email address stops existing after a staff change, and the contact form quietly breaks. The site looks fine. The submissions just go nowhere. The business blames "a quiet month" and keeps paying for ads.

A simple rule: once a quarter, test every form on your website with a real message from a real email account. If nothing arrives, find out why. Better yet, set up a monitoring tool that submits a test form every day and emails you when delivery fails.

## 7. Your mobile experience is an afterthought

In Ireland, most of your site's traffic will be phones. If your menu is tiny, the buttons are fiddly, the hero image crops out your call-to-action, or there's a "tap here to call" that doesn't trigger the dialler — you're losing people who would otherwise book.

This isn't about pixel-perfection. It's about the basics:

- Buttons big enough for a thumb
- Text legible without zooming
- Phone numbers clickable, not images
- Forms that auto-fill email and phone keyboards on mobile
- No horizontal scroll, ever

If you've never watched a customer actually try to use your site on a phone, do that before you do anything else. It's the fastest way to see what's wrong.

## 8. Your reviews and proof are invisible

Social proof does a lot of work you never see. If you've done 200 good jobs in the last three years and your site mentions none of them, every visitor treats you like a stranger.

Useful additions:

- Two or three short testimonials with real names (and towns, where appropriate)
- A handful of case studies — even two paragraphs each — showing what you did and what changed
- A Google Reviews badge, if you have the reviews
- Logos of well-known clients, with permission
