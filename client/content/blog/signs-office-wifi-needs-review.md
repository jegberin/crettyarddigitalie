# Signs your office Wi-Fi needs a review (and what to do first)

Bad office Wi-Fi is one of those problems every business quietly tolerates until somebody finally names it. Calls drop out of Teams at the same corner desk every day. The printer disconnects on Tuesday afternoons for no obvious reason. The shop floor staff switch off the office Wi-Fi on their phones because using 5G is faster. Nobody files a ticket — they just work around it.

The cost adds up all the same. Here's how to spot when your business Wi-Fi is overdue for a proper review, with the fixes I reach for first.

## 1. Teams and Zoom calls stutter in specific spots

Video calls are the best, cheapest test of a network. If Teams is clean in the boardroom but falls apart at the desks by the window, that's a coverage problem — the signal from your access point is too weak where you need it, not an internet problem. The broadband is fine; the last ten metres of delivery are the issue.

A site survey fixes this properly. That means walking the building with measuring tools and mapping where signal drops, where channels overlap, and where existing access points are in the wrong place. Cheap consumer routers can't give you this; proper business kit can.

## 2. The Wi-Fi works fine with two people and falls over with twelve

Home-grade routers are built for a handful of devices at once. Put fifteen staff laptops, a dozen phones, an EFTPOS terminal, a couple of smart TVs, and a printer on the same box, and it starts dropping connections even though the signal looks strong.

Business-class access points are engineered for density. They handle dozens of concurrent clients without degrading. If your office has grown past ten people on the same consumer-grade box, you'll feel this every day — it just won't get named.

## 3. Guest Wi-Fi is the same network as your business Wi-Fi

Giving clients the same SSID and password your staff use is a standard, easy mistake. It's also one of the biggest everyday security risks in an Irish SME. A guest's compromised laptop shouldn't be on the same network as your accounts server. A contractor's phone shouldn't need to be trusted like a staff device.

A sensible setup has a proper guest network — separate SSID, isolated from business traffic, bandwidth-capped so a visitor streaming YouTube can't flatten the call quality for the whole office. It takes an afternoon to set up and it costs nothing on most business-grade kit. More on this in the [network and Wi-Fi security](/network-wifi-security) service page.

## 4. There are dead zones — and everyone has mapped them

If your staff know where they have to stand to send an email, you have dead zones. Common causes:

- A single router at one end of a long office with thick walls between it and the other end
- Metal (filing cabinets, fire doors, a kitchen between two offices) absorbing signal
- An upstairs area covered by nothing but a bounced signal from downstairs
- A mezzanine or outbuilding with no dedicated access point

The fix is almost always the right number of access points in the right places, wired back to a single controller. Not three home routers on extension leads, all on overlapping channels, fighting each other.

## 5. You don't know who's connected

Ask your current IT person, or yourself: how many devices are on the Wi-Fi right now? Can you list them? Can you kick one off if a laptop goes missing?

If the answer is "I'd have to log into the router and scroll through a list of MAC addresses I don't recognise," then you don't really know what's on your network — and neither does anyone else. Business-grade Wi-Fi gives you a clean dashboard showing every device, who it belongs to, when it joined, and how much bandwidth it's using. That visibility is the first step to actually securing the network, not just hoping.

## 6. A printer, a card reader, or a till takes the whole thing down

When one device misbehaves and everybody's internet slows, something is wrong with the way your network is segmented. A well-designed office network separates traffic — staff devices on one VLAN, guests on another, payment terminals on a third, IoT devices on a fourth — so one badly-behaved printer can't chew up the bandwidth of every laptop in the building.

Segmentation also limits blast radius on the security side. If an attacker compromises the smart thermostat (yes, this happens), they shouldn't be able to pivot to the file server from there. Cheap consumer kit puts everything on one flat network. Business kit doesn't have to.

## 7. The equipment is more than five years old

Wi-Fi standards have moved on. Access points from 2018 are Wi-Fi 5 (802.11ac) and don't support the faster, lower-latency Wi-Fi 6 and 6E standards that modern laptops expect. They may still look like they work — but every client is running slower than it needs to, and coverage patterns are worse than they would be with newer hardware.

You don't always need to replace everything, but if your kit is five years old and your business has grown since then, a refresh is usually cheaper than another year of helpdesk tickets. Hardware in this space has dropped in price significantly; proper kit from a known brand is within reach for any SME that's serious about it.

## What a "proper review" actually involves

If two or three of the above sound familiar, the useful next step is a network review. For a small Irish business, that typically includes:

1. **A site walk** to map current access points, dead zones, and interference
2. **A look at the wiring closet** (or the cupboard where the patch panel lives) to see if the switch, firewall, and cabling are fit for purpose
3. **A device inventory** — what's on the network, what shouldn't be
4. **A check on SSIDs and guest isolation** — are staff, guest, and any other networks properly separated?
5. **A look at the firewall rules and remote access** — who can get in from outside, and how
6. **A written recommendations document** with priority and rough cost

It's the sort of thing that takes half a day for a small office and a full day for a larger one. You don't need to commit to any work up front — the point of the review is to tell you what to do and in what order, with prices, so you can decide.

## Quick wins while you wait

If a review isn't on the cards this month, a few things you can check yourself:

- **Change the default admin password** on your router. Many are still set to the factory default.
- **Turn off WPS.** Convenient for home use, but trivial to attack and not needed in a business.
- **Make sure you're on WPA3 or WPA2, never WEP or open.** WEP was broken before most of your staff were born.
- **Split guest Wi-Fi onto a different SSID** with a different password, and don't share that password with staff.
- **Reboot the kit once.** It shouldn't be necessary, but if you haven't restarted the router in six months, do it — and notice if the problems go away for a few days and then come back. That's a diagnostic in itself.

## What a decent setup looks like for an Irish SME

For a ten-to-thirty-person office in Ireland, the building blocks tend to look similar:

- A proper business firewall (not the router the broadband provider shipped)
- A managed switch with enough PoE ports to power your access points
- Two to four Wi-Fi 6 or 6E access points, placed by survey, not by guess
- Separate SSIDs for staff, guests, and IoT / operational devices
- A central controller or cloud dashboard you can actually use
- A maintenance plan that keeps firmware current
