# Web design tips for Irish professional services firms

A professional services firm's website is a different beast from a trades website or an e-commerce shop. Nobody hires a solicitor, an accountant, a clinic, or a consultant on impulse. The buyer's journey is longer, more careful, and more about trust than it is about price. The job of the website is to pass a slow, quiet inspection — and to make the first contact feel low-risk.

This post is about the things I've seen work, and the things I've seen quietly cost firms enquiries, on Irish professional services sites specifically. Accountancies in Laois, law firms in Kilkenny, dental practices in Carlow, small consultancies that sell on reputation. If that's your world, these are the levers worth pulling first.

## The buyer's question, underneath it all

Visitors to a professional services website are asking a single question in a handful of forms:

- "Is this the kind of firm I can trust with something important?"

Not "is their site pretty?" Not "are their services explained?" Just: can I trust them.

Every design and content decision should earn against that question. Anything that looks corporate-but-empty, or slick-but-anonymous, quietly fails the trust test even when it ticks every UX box. The firms who win on this are frequently the ones with the least flashy sites — but with visible people, clear language, and no marketing fluff.

## 1. Show real people, named, early

This is the single biggest differentiator on Irish professional services sites. Too many still use stock photos of generic professionals with stock smiles. Every buyer can spot those in two seconds. They don't build trust — they destroy a little of it.

The fix: real photos of real staff, properly lit, on an About page that actually says who the senior people are, where they qualified, what they've done, and what they're like to work with. For a small firm, a paragraph per person goes a long way. A short video from the founder — two minutes, unpolished, answering "why should someone hire us?" — does more work than a full glossy corporate homepage.

The worry professionals sometimes have ("I don't want to put my face on the internet") is worth naming. For 95% of firms, there's nothing to hide from, and hiding signals more risk than it reduces. If there's a specific concern, that's worth a separate conversation — but the default should be visibility.

## 2. Say what you specifically do, for whom

Professional services firms regularly write their home page as if it needs to appeal to everyone. A solicitor who does both commercial property and family law and will-writing and road traffic accidents, all described at the same depth, ends up looking like a generalist at everything and a specialist at nothing.

The fix: be specific. If 70% of the revenue is commercial property, the homepage should reflect that. The other practice areas get their own pages but don't need equal billing. Buyers looking for a specialist will land somewhere else if the first sentence doesn't match what they searched for.

A useful exercise: look at your top five revenue sources last year. Could a first-time visitor to your site, in 30 seconds, tell that those are your main things? If not, the homepage is miscalibrated.

## 3. Plain language beats credentials

Buyers of professional services respect expertise, but they've had their fill of jargon. A conveyancing page that reads "we handle the legal aspects of residential property transactions end-to-end" is fine. One that reads "we specialise in bespoke conveyancing solutions leveraging deep regulatory expertise" is worse than unhelpful — it actively signals distance.

Write like you speak to a good client in a meeting. Short sentences. Concrete examples. A piece of content that's useful to read — a plain explanation of, say, what happens in the first two weeks of a house purchase — is worth a full page of vague capability statements.

The other benefit: plain-language content gets found in Google. People don't search for "bespoke conveyancing solutions"; they search for "how long does buying a house take in Ireland". If your site has a clear answer to that question, you'll rank for it.

## 4. Case studies beat testimonials

Everybody puts testimonials on a professional services site. A smiling photo, a paragraph from "John, Kilkenny", and the usual "so helpful and professional". Most buyers have filtered these out unconsciously. They don't persuade anybody who's weighing up a €50,000 decision.

What does work is case studies, even short ones:

- What was the client's situation?
- What did the firm do?
- What was the outcome?

Three paragraphs, real enough to be specific (amounts, timelines, sector, even if the client is anonymised), and posted regularly enough that the site feels active. For a small firm, two or three genuine case studies are worth fifty generic testimonials. For sectors where confidentiality is tight — legal, medical — anonymised framings still work ("a construction client based in the Midlands, turnover around €3m…") as long as the story is specific.

## 5. Make the first step obvious and small

The first contact with a professional services firm is a big commitment in the buyer's head. They're worried about cost, they're worried about being pressured, they're worried about committing to something before they understand it. The website's job is to make the first step feel small.

What works:

- "Book a free 20-minute call" (defined scope — no pressure)
- "Request a callback" (less formal than a form)
- "Download a guide on X" (sign of competence, no commitment)
- A visible price range or fee structure, where possible — the firms who are opaque about prices often lose out to the ones who aren't

What doesn't:

- "Get a quote" with a form asking for twelve fields of personal information
- "Schedule a consultation" with no indication of cost
- No phone number, or a phone number hidden in the footer

A single, clear, low-commitment call-to-action on every page. Different verbs for different services, but always small and specific. You can see a version of this approach on our [how it works](/how-it-works) page.

## 6. Get your local search right

For an Irish professional services firm, most enquiries start with a Google search that includes your town or a neighbouring one. If your site isn't on page one for "solicitor Portlaoise" or "accountant Carlow town", you're relying on word-of-mouth alone — which caps growth.

Two things matter here:

- A Google Business Profile that's claimed, complete, and has recent reviews
- On-page content that reflects the towns and services you actually serve

Local service pages are a core tool — a page specifically about your services for clients in each of the towns you want to work in. Not fifteen pages, all near-duplicates. Three or four good ones, each with something genuinely different to say. ## 7. Keep the site boringly reliable

The slowest-moving thing about a professional services firm's reputation is accumulated small signals of care. A fast site. Forms that actually work. A contact page where the phone number is right. A copyright date in the current year. No broken images, no "Lorem ipsum" lurking in a sidebar because somebody forgot to remove it.

Buyers scan for signs that a firm pays attention to detail, because they're hiring you to pay attention to detail. A site that gives off signals of neglect — even small ones — costs trust disproportionately. This is the whole argument for a [website care plan](/website-care-plans): not dramatic monthly changes, but small, regular maintenance that keeps the site quietly professional.

## 8. Don't over-design

There's a strong temptation among smaller firms to "get a proper designer in" and end up with a site that's all animation, oversized type, and dramatic scroll effects. That's rarely what wins the buyer.

For professional services, a clean, legible, calm design almost always beats something flashy. Good typography. Plenty of white space. Fast load. Clear navigation. No pop-up asking for an email before they've read anything. This isn't a visual timidity argument — it's that the content and the person behind it are what the buyer is actually evaluating. Design's job is to stay out of the way of that.

## 9. Compliance details, visible but quiet

Professional services firms carry regulatory obligations — professional body registration, GDPR, in some cases AML requirements, professional indemnity insurance. These should appear on the site in the footer or an About page: law society number, accountancy body membership, clinic registration. Not because buyers read them often, but because their absence is conspicuous when present in competitors' sites.

One sentence in the footer is usually enough. Don't make it a wall.

## Where to start if you're redoing a site

Three things, in order:

1. **Rewrite the homepage first sentence.** One line, in plain English, stating what you do and for whom. Share it around the team before touching anything else.
2. **Commit to one good photo of each senior person on the team.** The rest of the site can iterate around those.
3. **Write one real case study.** One. Use it as the reference for format; write more over time.

If the first sentence is clear, the people are visible, and there's one genuine story of work you've done, you're ahead of most Irish firms in your sector. Everything else is iteration on that foundation.
